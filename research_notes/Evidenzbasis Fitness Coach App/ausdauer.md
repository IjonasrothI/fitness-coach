# Endurance Training (Running & Cycling) for Trained Recreational Adults: Evidence Base for App Rules

Scope: trained recreational adults, health and continuous-improvement goals, typically 3–5 h/week. Evidence strength labels used below:
- **[A]** meta-analysis / large prospective cohort with consistent findings
- **[B]** single RCTs, smaller cohorts, or meta-analyses with important heterogeneity or limitations
- **[C]** expert consensus, narrative review, mechanistic reasoning, consumer tests
- **[ELITE]** evidence comes mainly or only from elite or highly trained athletes
- **[UNVERIFIED]** citation details come from background knowledge and were not re-checked against a source during this research session. The report writer should verify these before final citation.

## 1. Training intensity distribution (polarized vs pyramidal vs threshold)

### Takeaway
Across meta-analyses, polarized (POL) training has at best a small, short-term advantage for VO2peak and no proven advantage for time-trial performance. The best available individual-participant-data analysis (Rosenblat 2025) suggests that **recreational athletes may do better with pyramidal (PYR)** and competitive athletes with POL. For a 3–5 h/week user, a pyramidal distribution (mostly easy, some threshold/tempo, a little high intensity) is the defensible default **[B]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca 2024**, *Sports Medicine* 54(8):2071–2095, DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z. Systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies (n = 437). POL improved VO2peak more than other TIDs, mainly in interventions under 12 weeks. There was no POL superiority for other endurance performance surrogates (e.g., time trials, thresholds). The review did not restrict by athlete level (elite, professional or recreational) — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z.pdf); [PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428)
- **Rosenblat et al. 2025**, "Which Training Intensity Distribution Intervention will Produce the Greatest Improvements in Maximal Oxygen Uptake and Time-Trial Performance in Endurance Athletes? A Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis of Individual Participant Data", *Sports Medicine* 55(3):655–673, DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3. 13 studies, n = 348 (150 recreational, 198 competitive; 296 male, 52 female). Using time in HR zone, POL vs PYR showed no overall difference in VO2max (SMD −0.06, p = 0.68) or time-trial performance (SMD −0.05, p = 0.34), and no significant difference between POL and any other TID. In a subgroup analysis, recreational vs competitive athletes responded differently to POL vs PYR for VO2max (SMD −0.63, p < 0.05). The authors suggest competitive athletes may benefit from POL and recreational athletes from PYR — [Univ. Stirling record](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/2095779); [Full text PDF mirror](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Which-Training-Intensity-Distribution-Intervention-will.pdf)
- Sample size is small and women are underrepresented (52 of 348), so the recreational-PYR recommendation should be treated as tentative — [Rosenblat 2025 (Stirling)](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/2095779)
- A clinical trial registered in 2025/26 is comparing polarized vs threshold TID for race time and body composition in recreational runners aged 20–45 (NCT07498608). This shows the question is still open for recreational runners — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07498608](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07498608)
- Earlier work (background, [UNVERIFIED] details):
  - Rosenblat MA, Perrotta AS, Vicenzino B. Polarized vs. threshold training intensity distribution on endurance sport performance: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *J Strength Cond Res* 2019;33(12):3491–3500. It reported that POL beat threshold training for time-trial performance in interventions of 6 weeks or more, in trained athletes. **[B]**
  - Casado A, González-Mohíno F, González-Ravé JM, Foster C. Training periodization, methods, intensity distribution, and volume in highly trained and elite distance runners: a systematic review. *IJSPP* 2022;17(6):820–833, DOI 10.1123/ijspp.2021-0435. It describes elite runners as mostly pyramidal, with a shift toward polarized near competition. **[ELITE]** Not found in this session's searches.
  - The descriptive "~80/20" distribution comes from elite and highly trained observational data (Seiler & Kjerland 2006, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*; Stöggl & Sperlich 2014, *Front Physiol*). **[ELITE]** [UNVERIFIED]

### Inferences
- **Rule (default TID) [B]:** For most weeks, use a pyramidal distribution. About 75–85% of sessions or time should be easy (below the first threshold, "can talk in full sentences"), about 10–20% moderate/threshold, and about 5–10% high intensity (above the second threshold). At 3–5 h/week, this usually means 1–2 "quality" sessions per week, with the rest easy.
- **Rule [C]:** Optionally offer short polarized blocks (under 12 weeks) for users who want to raise VO2max. This is the only setting where the meta-analytic signal for POL exists (Oliveira 2024).
- With low weekly volume, the absolute amount of high intensity matters more than percentages. 80/20 at 3 h/week leaves only about 36 min of hard work, which is still in line with HIIT dose evidence (see Section 6). Percentages from elite athletes training 15–25 h/week should not be copied directly. **[C]**
- TID can be counted by sessions or by time in zone. Rosenblat 2025 used time in HR zone. The app should state which method it uses.

### Gaps
- No high-quality RCT tests TID specifically in 3–5 h/week recreational adults with health outcomes as the endpoint.
- The Silva Oliveira paper named in the brief appears to be the Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca 2024 *Sports Medicine* review. No separate "Silva Oliveira" meta-analysis was identified.
- Casado 2022 details were not verified this session.

## 2. Zone models and zone determination with consumer devices

### Takeaway
Age-based HRmax formulas have errors of about ±7–12 bpm (SEE). Tanaka (208 − 0.7 × age) and Gellish (207 − 0.7 × age) perform best among the common formulas and clearly better than 220 − age. A measured HRmax, or better, anchors from thresholds (talk test, 30-min test, critical speed/power, FTP) should replace formula estimates once data exist **[A/B]**. Watch VO2max estimates are useful for tracking trends, not as absolute values **[B]**.

### Cited Findings — HRmax formulas
- In a validation study of 99 treadmill tests, the Fox, Gellish, Arena and Tanaka equations showed minimal bias and similar limits of agreement. Gulati, Åstrand, Nes and Fairbarn (male) differed significantly from measured HRmax. RMSE was about 10.7 bpm for both Gellish and Tanaka. Nes had a bias of about −4.9 bpm and RMSE of about 11.8 bpm — [IJES 2020, PMC7523886](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7523886); [Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7523886/table/t2-ijes-13-7-1242)
- Age-only formulas are generally cited with a standard error of estimate of about 7–12 bpm. In physically active people, Tanaka gave the second-best prediction, while 220 − age had mean errors up to about 9 bpm — [Frontiers in Physiology 2021, 10.3389/fphys.2021.695950](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.695950/full); [PMC8362801](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362801)
- In marathon runners, Tanaka overestimated HRmax in women by about 5 bpm. The authors recommend Tanaka for male marathon runners — [PMC8362801](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362801)
- A large validation study (n = 2,449) refit age-based equations. The correlations with observed HRmax were only 0.64 (men) and 0.664 (women), so most individual variance is not explained by age — [PubMed 25389634](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25389634/)
- Original equations ([UNVERIFIED] citation details):
  - Tanaka H, Monahan KD, Seals DR. Age-predicted maximal heart rate revisited. *J Am Coll Cardiol* 2001;37(1):153–156, DOI 10.1016/S0735-1097(00)01054-8. Formula: 208 − 0.7 × age.
  - Gellish RL et al. Longitudinal modeling of the relationship between age and maximal heart rate. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2007;39(5):822–829. Formula: 207 − 0.7 × age.
  - Nes BM et al. Age-predicted maximal heart rate in healthy subjects: the HUNT Fitness Study. *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 2013;23(6):697–704. Formula: 211 − 0.64 × age.

### Cited Findings — field threshold tests
- **Talk test.** In a systematic review (mainly cardiopulmonary patients, mostly coronary artery disease), the last stage where talking was still comfortable matched the first ventilatory threshold (VT1). Workload, VO2 and HR at that stage did not differ from VT1. Validity was supported, but only one study had an adequate reliability analysis — Vieira et al. 2022, *Reviews in Cardiovascular Medicine* 23(7):225, DOI 10.31083/j.rcm2307225 — [PMC11266803](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11266803/)
- A 2011 study (n = 15) found that talk-test outcomes related more closely to the lactate threshold than to the ventilatory threshold — [PubMed 21774751](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21774751/). A study in well-trained cyclists also examined the talk test against thresholds — [PubMed 23007491](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23007491/) (results not retrieved).
- **Critical speed (CS).** A 2025 systematic review (19 of 450 studies included) found the time-trial method and the 3-min all-out test to be the most common field methods for CS in runners. Both showed high reliability under specific conditions — *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* 2025, DOI 10.3389/fspor.2025.1520914 — [PMC11933073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11933073/)
- In trained runners, CS from two time trials gave a "reasonable, albeit higher" estimate than three trials — [medRxiv 2023.07.04.23292118](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.04.23292118.full.pdf)
- Trial duration biases CS. Short trials (about 1 min) push CS up and D′ down. Long trials (about 30 min) push CS down and D′ up (triathlete data) — [Kent repository](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/11314)
- **Cycling FTP from a 20-min test.** In Borszcz et al. 2019 (*IJSPP*), FTP (95% of 20-min power) had a bias of 1.4% ± 9.2% (95% LoA) versus MLSS, a typical error of about 4.7%, and r = 0.91 — [PubMed 30676826](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30676826/); [PMC6886609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6886609)
- In contrast, Lillo-Beviá et al. 2022 found the 20-min TT very repeatable (SEM ±3 W, smallest detectable change ±9 W) but biased 26 ± 7 W above MLSS. They recommend revising the FTP calculation — [Frontiers in Physiology, 10.3389/fphys.2020.613151 (PDF)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.613151/pdf)
- See also "Is the Functional Threshold Power a Valid Metric to Estimate the Maximal Lactate Steady State in Cyclists?" — [PubMed 31714462](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31714462/)
- In a 2025 preprint (not peer reviewed), FTP20 was reliable, but riders held 95% of FTP20 for only 42 ± 17 min, not the 60 min that FTP is supposed to represent — [Preprints.org 202502.0505](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202502.0505)
- No validation evidence was found that ramp-test-derived FTP (e.g., 75% of the peak 1-min power) is a valid MLSS surrogate — [summary of search](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202502.0505) (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings — device accuracy
- **Apple Watch VO2max.** Lambe et al. 2025, *PLoS ONE* 20(5):e0323741 (n = 30; 5–10 days of wear, then a treadmill VO2max test with indirect calorimetry). The watch underestimated VO2max by a mean of 6.07 mL/kg/min (95% CI 3.77–8.38). Limits of agreement ran from −6.11 to +18.26 mL/kg/min. The authors conclude refinement is needed before clinical use — [PubMed 40373042](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40373042/); [PMC12080799](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12080799/)
- Caserman et al. 2024, *JMIR Biomedical Engineering* 9:e59459. Validation of Apple Watch Series 7 VO2max (n = 19) against a metabolic gas analyzer. The paper raises concerns about bias and limited representativeness in Apple's own validation. Numerical results were not retrieved — [JMIR Biomed Eng 2024](https://biomedeng.jmir.org/2024/1/e59459)
- **Apple Watch HR, steps, energy.** A 2025 meta-analysis (Choe & Kang, Univ. of Mississippi; 56 studies; reportedly in *Healthcare*) gave mean absolute percentage errors of 4.43% for HR, 8.17% for steps, and 27.96% for energy expenditure. The energy error appeared across walking, running, cycling and mixed workouts. Note: this was found through a press release and poster, and the journal listing was not verified — [Ole Miss news 2025](https://olemiss.edu/news/2025/6/apple-watch-accuracy-study/index.html); [eGrove poster](https://egrove.olemiss.edu/ored_showcase/2025/posters/10)
- Wrist-worn devices lose accuracy during intense exercise — [Sensors 2023, PMC9952291](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9952291/)
- **COROS.** No peer-reviewed validation study of COROS HR, GPS or VO2max was found. A planned validation framework exists, but it has no results yet — [Fort Hays State scholarly project 2026](https://scholars.fhsu.edu/sacad/vol2026/iss2026/46)
- Consumer tests of COROS (**[C]**, small samples):
  - The Pace 3's optical HR was less accurate than competitors when HR changed quickly during intervals — [Notebookcheck on the5krunner test](https://www.notebookcheck.net/New-study-shows-problems-with-Coros-Pace-3-smartwatch-heart-rate-accuracy.779464.0.html)
  - Another test measured ±1.54 bpm versus a Polar H10 on a short steady run — [OutdoorGearLab](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/reviews/camping-and-hiking/gps-watch/coros-pace-3)
  - The Pace Pro lagged during rapid HR changes (1–8 bpm low) and read 10–20 bpm high on a hike. GPS was rated good to very good — [Android Central](https://www.androidcentral.com/wearables/coros-pace-pro-gps-and-hr-accuracy-test)

### Inferences
- **Rule (HRmax default) [A/B]:** If no measured value exists, use Tanaka (208 − 0.7 × age), and show the user an uncertainty of about ±10 bpm. Never use 220 − age. Prompt the user to replace the estimate with an observed maximum (e.g., the highest HR from a chest-strap-recorded hard effort), or better with threshold-based zones.
- **Rule (zone anchoring hierarchy) [B/C]:** Prefer zones anchored to measured thresholds over zones based on a percentage of HRmax. Suggested order:
  1. Lab test
  2. Field test: CS from 2 time trials (e.g., about 3 and 12 min, or 1,200 m and 3,600 m), FTP/CP for cycling, or a 30-min running TT (average HR of the last 20 min ≈ LTHR [UNVERIFIED convention])
  3. Talk test for the VT1 upper limit of the easy zone
  4. HR reserve (Karvonen) using resting HR plus an estimated HRmax
  5. Percentage of HRmax only as a fallback
- **Rule (easy-zone ceiling) [B]:** Define "easy" with the talk test, i.e., the user can still speak comfortably in full sentences. This works without devices, is valid against VT1/LT1, and also guards against HR-sensor errors.
- **Rule (CS test timing) [B]:** Use time trials of about 3 to 15 min. Very short trials push CS up. Warn that 2-trial CS slightly overestimates CS, so set threshold-session paces at or slightly below CS.
- **Rule (FTP) [B]:** Treat 95% of 20-min power as an estimate with about ±5–9% error. It may overestimate MLSS in some riders. Offer a check: if the rider cannot hold about 40–60 min at the computed FTP, reduce it by about 3–5% [C]. The retest interval should be at least 4–8 weeks, because changes below about 9 W are within measurement noise (Lillo-Beviá SDC).
- **Rule (watch VO2max) [B]:** Show watch VO2max as a trend line with a smoothing window, not as an absolute number. Do not compare values between brands. For Apple Watch, expect underestimation in fitter users (bias about −6 mL/kg/min, with wide LoA).
- **Rule (HR source) [B/C]:** For interval sessions, recommend a chest strap. Optical HR lags during rapid changes. Hold off on auto-adjusting intensity from optical HR in the first 60–90 s of intervals.
- **Rule (energy expenditure) [B]:** Do not use watch kcal for nutrition prescription without strong caveats (about 28% MAPE for Apple Watch).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed validation of COROS HR, GPS or VO2max was found (2020–2026).
- No GPS pace validation studies were retrieved for Apple Watch or COROS.
- No validation of running power (Stryd, Apple, COROS) was searched in this session. This needs a follow-up search.
- No validity evidence was found for ramp-test FTP (e.g., 75% of the peak 1-min power).
- The validity of the 30-min running test (Friel LTHR) was not verified in this session.
- Caserman 2024 numeric results were not retrieved.

## 3. Volume progression and load monitoring

### Takeaway
Session-RPE (RPE × minutes) is a valid, device-free internal load measure across exercise types **[A]**. The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) has serious conceptual and statistical flaws. In recreational runners it was **not** associated with injury, or was even inversely associated **[A/B]**. Monotony and strain are plausible but weakly validated **[C]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Foster et al. 2001**, "A new approach to monitoring exercise training", *J Strength Cond Res* 15(1):109–115 ([UNVERIFIED] volume and pages). Session load = CR-10 RPE × duration in minutes. It was validated against HR-based load during non-steady-state and prolonged exercise. Validity was established across a wide variety of exercise types. The CR-10 anchors were modified (e.g., "strong" became "hard") — [Record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c340t-c8f915026da85068cb7b56ce4173817739b27d08a54ec2f340795a437b4c184e2); [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2017 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00612/full)
- A 2017 mini-review (Haddad et al., *Frontiers in Neuroscience* 11:612, DOI 10.3389/fnins.2017.00612) identified 36 studies. These confirmed the validity, good reliability and internal consistency of session-RPE across many sports — [Frontiers 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00612/full)
- **Monotony and strain.** Monotony = weekly mean daily load ÷ SD of daily load. Strain = weekly load × monotony. High monotony combined with high load has been linked to overtraining onset, and high strain to illness and poor performance — [Frontiers 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00612/full)
- **Impellizzeri et al. 2020**, "Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio: Conceptual Issues and Fundamental Pitfalls", *IJSPP* 15(6):907–913. ACWR has no established causal link to injury. It does not properly normalize the numerator by the denominator, it is ambiguous, it adds noise, and it creates statistical artifacts. Its use can lead to inappropriate recommendations — [UTS repository](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/145169)
- Impellizzeri et al. 2020, "Training Load and Its Role in Injury Prevention, Part 2: Conceptual and Methodologic Pitfalls", *J Athl Train* — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938)
- Impellizzeri et al. 2021, "What Role Do Chronic Workloads Play in the Acute to Chronic Workload Ratio? Time to Dismiss ACWR and Its Underlying Theory", *Sports Medicine* 51:581–592 (online December 2020), DOI 10.1007/s40279-020-01378-6. Re-analysis of published data showed that neither ACWR nor acute load gave meaningful predictive advantage over an intercept-only model. The authors recommend dismissing ACWR — [Univ. Verona PDF](https://iris.univr.it/retrieve/e34cfb98-e922-4c2f-aab5-18583ab7e31b/Impellizzeri_What%20Role%20Do%20Chronic%20Workloads%20Play_SportMed_2021.pdf)
- A 2021 Frontiers editorial ("Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio: Is There Scientific Evidence?") summarizes substantial criticism by Impellizzeri et al. 2020a,b and Wang et al. 2020 — [PMC8138569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569)
- Wang C, Vargas JT, Stokes T, Steele R, Shrier I. Analyzing activity and injury: lessons learned from the acute:chronic workload ratio. *Sports Med* 2020;50:1243–1254 — [UNVERIFIED]. It was not retrieved this session, but it is cited in the editorial above.
- **In recreational runners (Garmin-RUNSAFE, 2025):** ACWR and week-to-week gradual load changes showed no association with injury, and in some cases an inverse one. The authors advise caution about relying on these calculations — [PMC12421110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110). In the BJSM 5,200-runner analysis, ACWR spikes and week-to-week spikes showed no clear association — [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751)
- Lead author R.Ø. Nielsen states that the ACWR-based injury guidance built into sports watches is poorly grounded — [EurekAlert press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1090184)

### Inferences
- **Rule (internal load) [A]:** Ask for a CR-10 session RPE after every session (about 30 min post-session, per Foster convention [UNVERIFIED timing]). Store session load = RPE × minutes. This works the same for running and cycling, so mixed-mode weekly load can be computed.
- **Rule (no ACWR gating) [A/B]:** Do not use ACWR "sweet spot" thresholds (0.8–1.3) as hard rules or as injury-risk warnings. At most, show weekly load trends for information.
- **Rule (monotony) [C]:** Flag weeks where monotony is above about 2.0 together with rising load (a Foster-derived convention [UNVERIFIED threshold]). Respond by suggesting more variation in day-to-day load (true easy or rest days). This is a soft nudge, not a block.
- **Rule (progression) [C]:** For weekly volume progression, there is no evidence-based percentage. Progress based on how the user tolerates training (RPE trend, soreness, pain reports), and above all cap single-session distance relative to recent maxima (see Section 4).
- TRIMP variants (Banister, Edwards, Lucia) need reliable HR data. Session-RPE is the simpler default. Offer HR-TRIMP as a secondary measure where a chest strap is available. **[C]**

### Gaps
- TRIMP variant validation literature (2020–2026) was not retrieved in this session.
- The Wang 2020 content was not verified.
- No validated monotony or strain cut-offs exist for recreational endurance athletes.

## 4. Running injury prevention

### Takeaway
The strongest recent signal is at the level of the **single session**. A run that is more than 10% longer than the longest run in the previous 30 days is associated with higher overuse-injury rates (HRR 1.52–2.28). Weekly changes and ACWR were not associated with injury **[A/B, one large cohort, needs replication]**. The "10% rule" for weekly mileage is not evidence-based. Nielsen 2014 suggests keeping 2-week increases under 30% in novices, but the overall result was non-significant **[B]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Frandsen et al. 2025:** Schuster Brandt Frandsen J, Hulme A, Parner ET, Møller M, Lindman I, Abrahamson J, Simonsen NS, Jacobsen JS, Ramskov D, Skejø S, Malisoux L, Bertelsen ML, Nielsen RØ. "How much running is too much? Identifying high-risk running sessions in a 5200-person cohort study." *Br J Sports Med* 2025;59(17):1203–1210 — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/59/17/1203); [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751); [LIH portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/how-much-running-is-too-much-identifying-high-risk-running-sessio/)
  - Design and sample: prospective 18-month cohort of 5,205 adult recreational runners from 87 countries, about 588,000 Garmin-recorded sessions. More than 35% self-reported a running-related injury, mostly overuse.
  - Exposure: each session's distance compared with the longest run in the previous 30 days. Categories: ≤10% (reference, including regressions), >10–30% (small spike), >30–100% (moderate spike), >100% (large spike).
  - Results: HRR 1.64 (small), 1.52 (moderate), 2.28 (large spike). ACWR and week-to-week spikes showed no clear association.
  - Limitations: self-reported injury, observational design, and no biomechanical confounders. The blog notes that even 1–10% increases may carry some risk, so the authors call for replication.
  - Confidence intervals were not shown on the blog page — [physiotutors summary](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/running-injury-prevention/)
- **Garmin-RUNSAFE descriptive:** 7,605 runners in 87 countries over 18 months. Cumulative injury was about 57.6% at 1,000 km and 69.8% at 2,000 km of running, with large between-country differences — *JOSPT* 2024 (online November 2023) — [PubMed 37970820](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37970820/)
- Equipment analysis in the same cohort: 3,713 of 7,347 runners (51%) were injured over 18 months — [PMC12373465](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12373465)
- The press release reports that injury risk rises as an individual run gets longer than usual — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1090184)
- **Nielsen et al. 2014**, "Excessive progression in weekly running distance and risk of running-related injuries: an association which varies according to type of injury", *JOSPT* 44(10):739–747. One-year prospective cohort of novices.
  - The overall injury rate did not differ between progression groups. For >30% vs <10% per week over 2 weeks: HR 1.59, 95% CI 0.96–2.66, p = 0.07.
  - "Distance-related" injuries (patellofemoral pain, IT band syndrome, among others) were more common with more than 30% progression. The authors advise novices to keep increases under 30% per week over a 2-week period — [AAU record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn); [JOSPT PDF](https://www.orthobethesda.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/jospt-running-increased-mileage.pdf)
- **Damsted et al. 2019** (half-marathon preparation, ProjectRun21): more runners were injured with weekly distance increases of 20–60% than with increases under 20% — [LIH record](https://events.lih.lu/crp/publication/the-association-between-changes-in-weekly-running-distance-and-running-related-injury-preparing-for-a-half-marathon-11906)
- **10% rule:** a systematic review concluded the popular "10% rule" for weekly distance increases is not justified — [PMC4842282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842282) (as summarized in search results; the full text was not opened)

### Inferences
- **Rule (single-session cap) [B, strongest available]:** Track a rolling "longest run in the last 30 days" (by distance). Warn when a planned run exceeds it by more than 10%. Strongly warn, or require an explicit override, when it exceeds it by more than 30%. Never plan more than +100% (e.g., a first long run after a break). When building a long run, step up by ≤10% each time and keep a buffer.
- **Rule (weekly change) [B/C]:** Do not use a hard 10% weekly rule. As a soft guideline, keep 2-week cumulative volume increases under about 20–30%. Watch for runners prone to knee and ITB problems.
- **Rule (returning from a break) [C]:** After a gap of 30 days or more, the 30-day longest run resets to a low value. Restart with a conservative long run, which the single-session rule handles automatically.
- **Rule (pain reporting) [C]:** Include a quick pain/niggle check-in. Because overuse injuries make up the majority, a reported recurring pain should trigger a volume and intensity reduction. This is consensus-based.
- Expect high base rates. About half of recreational runners report an injury over 18 months, so the app should frame injury prevention as risk reduction, not elimination. **[A, descriptive]**

### Gaps
- Confidence intervals for Frandsen 2025 HRRs were not retrieved. The non-monotonic pattern (small spike 1.64 > moderate 1.52) suggests imprecision.
- No equivalent single-session evidence exists for cycling, which has a much lower overuse-injury profile.
- Other risk factors (previous injury, BMI, sex, footwear, running experience) were not searched in this session. Previous injury is widely cited as the strongest predictor, but this is [UNVERIFIED] here.
- The source of the 10%-rule systematic review was not fully verified.

## 5. Concurrent training / interference effect

### Takeaway
For health-oriented recreational adults, adding endurance training does **not** meaningfully blunt strength or muscle-size gains **[A]**. There is a small fiber-level interference with running but not with cycling **[B]**. Explosive/power outcomes may be blunted **[B]**. VO2max gains are reduced only in untrained individuals **[A]**. If strength and endurance are in the same session, doing strength first helps lower-body strength. Order does not affect VO2max **[A/B]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann M, Feuerbacher JF, Sünkeler M, et al. 2022**, "Compatibility of Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training for Skeletal Muscle Size and Function: An Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", *Sports Medicine* 52:601–612 (online 2021). Adding aerobic training to strength training did not compromise gains in muscle hypertrophy or maximal strength. Results held across aerobic modality (cycling vs running), weekly frequency, training status and age — [JCU repository](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/); [PubMed 34878640](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34878640/); [Preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/37/71/54)
  - The preprint notes this updates Wilson 2012, which found that explosive power was blunted by concurrent training — [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/37/71/54)
- **Lundberg TR, Feuerbacher JF, Sünkeler M, Schumann M. 2022**, "The Effects of Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training on Muscle Fiber Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", *Sports Medicine* 52:2391–2403. There was a small interference effect on fiber hypertrophy, mainly in type I fibers, when the aerobic training was running rather than cycling (SMD −0.81). Other subgroup comparisons showed no difference — [PMC9474354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474354)
- **2024 *Sports Medicine* meta-analysis** (59 studies, n = 1,346; DOI 10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9). Concurrent strength + endurance training reduced VO2max gains only in untrained people. Trained and highly trained athletes showed no reduction — [PubMed 37847373](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37847373/); [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/concurrent-strength-and-endurance-training-a-systematic-review-an/)
- **Eddens L, van Someren K, Howatson G. 2018**, "The Role of Intra-Session Exercise Sequence in the Interference Effect: A Systematic Review with Meta-Analysis", *Sports Medicine* 48(1):177–188. Ten studies, interventions of 5 weeks or more, healthy adults. Resistance before endurance improved lower-body dynamic strength (WMD 6.91%, 95% CI 1.96–11.87). There was no effect of order on hypertrophy, static strength, VO2max or body fat — [PubMed 28917030](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28917030/)
- **Murlasits et al. 2018, *J Sports Sci*:** strength before endurance benefits lower-body strength, and aerobic capacity is not affected by order — [PubMed 28783467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- Another meta-analysis reports that impairment appears mainly when resistance and endurance training are done less than about 20 min apart (same session), not when separated by more than about 2 hours — [Springer, s40279-021-01426-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01426-9); [PMC8053170](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170). The exact attribution of this claim to a specific paper was not fully verified.

### Inferences
- **Rule (compatibility messaging) [A]:** Tell users that combining strength and endurance training is compatible with health and strength goals. Do not discourage concurrent training.
- **Rule (sequencing) [A/B]:**
  - If both happen in one session and lower-body strength is a priority: strength first, then endurance.
  - If endurance performance is the priority, order hardly matters for VO2max. Put the priority modality first.
  - Prefer a gap of about 3 hours or more, or separate days, between hard leg strength sessions and hard endurance sessions. A gap of 2 hours or more removes most of the measured interference, and 6–24 hours is the conventional recommendation [C].
- **Rule (modality) [B]:** When a user's priority is muscle mass, the app can suggest cycling over running for the aerobic component (less fiber-level interference, lower eccentric load).
- **Rule (scheduling) [C]:** Avoid hard lower-body strength work within about 24 h before key run quality sessions or long runs. This is a fatigue-management convention, not directly tested in recreational runners.
- **Rule (power) [B]:** If the user values explosive power, keep high-velocity strength work separate from long endurance sessions.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 RCTs were found that test hour-specific gaps (e.g., 3 h vs 6 h vs 24 h) in recreational adults.
- No direct comparison of cycling vs running interference on endurance outcomes was retrieved.

## 6. Minimum effective dose, health guidelines, VO2max as a health marker, HIIT and Zone 2

### Takeaway
WHO 2020 recommends 150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min/week vigorous activity, plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days **[A]**. Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is a strong, dose-dependent predictor of mortality. Each +1 MET is associated with about 11–17% lower all-cause mortality **[A, observational]**. HIIT improves VO2max equal to or more than continuous training, especially with longer intervals (≥2 min) and adequate volume **[A]**. The claim that "Zone 2 is the optimal intensity for mitochondria" is not supported **[B/C]**.

### Cited Findings
- **Bull FC, Al-Ansari SS, Biddle S, et al. 2020**, "World Health Organization 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour", *Br J Sports Med* 54(24):1451–1462, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955. Adults should do 150–300 min of moderate or 75–150 min of vigorous aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent combination, plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days per week. They should also limit sedentary time — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451?s=09); [PMC7719906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7719906/); [University of Sydney news](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2020/11/26/new-global-guidelines-on-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behavio.html)
- **Lang JJ, Prince SA, Merucci K, et al. 2024**, "Cardiorespiratory fitness is a strong and consistent predictor of morbidity and mortality among adults: an overview of meta-analyses representing over 20.9 million observations from 199 unique cohort studies", *Br J Sports Med* 58(10):556–566, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2023-107849 ([UNVERIFIED] volume, pages and DOI). Each 1-MET higher CRF was associated with HR 0.89 (0.86–0.92) and HR 0.83 (0.78–0.88) for all-cause mortality, i.e., 11–17% lower risk. For heart failure, HR was 0.82 (0.79–0.84). High vs low CRF gave HR 0.47 (0.39–0.56) for all-cause mortality — [PubMed 38599681](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38599681/); [PMC11103301](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11103301); [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11115)
- A 2025 meta-analysis from the same group (42 studies) gave a pooled RR of 0.86 (0.83–0.88) for all-cause and 0.84 (0.80–0.87) for CVD mortality per higher MET — [Adelaide record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/91ffcf0f-9c9d-433e-b51e-eb1529c96855)
- **HIIT and VO2max:**
  - An overview of systematic reviews (*Sports Medicine* 2024; "The Effect of Exercise Training Intensity on VO2max in Healthy Adults") found small-to-moderate benefits of high-intensity over lower-intensity training. The benefit depends on training variables beyond intensity — [PMC11022784](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11022784/)
  - Wen et al. 2019 (*J Sports Sci*): long intervals (≥2 min), high volume (≥15 min of work) and programs of 4–12 weeks or longer gave significantly larger VO2max effects — [PubMed 30733142](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30733142/)
  - A 2023 *Sports Medicine* meta-analysis of HIIT in healthy adults found VO2max gains of +3.50 mL/kg/min in men and +3.34 mL/kg/min in women, with no significant sex differences in the primary analysis — [Springer, s40279-023-01914-0](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-023-01914-0)
  - Weston et al. 2014 (*Sports Medicine*): low-volume HIT improved fitness in adults — [Strathprints PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/87212/1/Weston-etal-SM-2014-Effects-of-low-volume-high-intensity-interval-training-HIT-on-fitness-in-adults.pdf). Low-volume HIIT also improved CRF more than both no exercise and MICT across normal-weight, overweight and obese adults — [Springer, s40279-019-01167-w](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-019-01167-w)
  - A 2024/25 meta-analysis of 115 trials compared HIIT with MICT ("One Size Doesn't Fit All"). Its headline pooled result was not retrieved — [Swansea Cronfa PDF](https://cronfa.swan.ac.uk/Record/cronfa71602/Download/71602__36380__213077f261854fbe8476ac73cfa788f8.pdf)
- **Zone 2:** Storoschuk KL, Gurd BJ, et al. 2025, "Much Ado About Zone 2: A Narrative Review Assessing the Efficacy of Zone 2 Training for Improving Mitochondrial Capacity and Cardiorespiratory Fitness in the General Population", *Sports Medicine* ([UNVERIFIED] exact title wording).
  - Current evidence does not support Zone 2 as the optimal intensity for improving mitochondrial or fatty-acid oxidative capacity in the general population. The case for Zone 2 rests largely on extrapolation from high-volume elite athletes.
  - Few studies actually prescribe Zone 2. Higher intensities also improve these capacities, sometimes more.
  - Definitions of "Zone 2" range from about 30% to 80% of VO2max.
  - Sources: [PubMed 40560504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560504/); [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/display/publication3529717); [Sci-Sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/zone-2-the-ideal-intensity-myth-or-scientific-reality-255)

### Inferences
- **Rule (health floor) [A]:**
  - The app's weekly minimum target is at least 150 min moderate-equivalent. Count vigorous minutes double (WHO 2:1 equivalence).
  - Add 2 strength days.
  - A 3–5 h/week user already exceeds the WHO upper range, so frame the goal as maintaining that and raising CRF, not as meeting a minimum.
- **Rule (VO2max as a progress KPI) [A, observational]:** Use estimated VO2max (trend) as the primary health-improvement metric. Explain the meaning of +1 MET (= +3.5 mL/kg/min ≈ 11–17% lower all-cause mortality risk, associational). Pair it with a device-error caveat (Section 2).
- **Rule (time-efficient VO2max work) [A]:**
  - Include 1 HIIT session per week by default, and 2 at most for users at 3–5 h/week.
  - Prefer long intervals (e.g., 4–6 × 3–4 min at about 90–95% HRmax, or 4 × 4 min "Norwegian" format [C for exact format]) with at least 15 min of total work.
  - Program blocks of 4–12 weeks or longer.
- **Rule (SIT/short HIIT) [B]:** Offer low-volume HIIT/SIT as a "minimum effective dose" option for busy weeks. It still improves CRF better than nothing, and comparably to MICT.
- **Rule (Zone 2 framing) [B/C]:** Present easy/Zone 2 training as the volume and recovery base that makes high weekly volume tolerable. Do not present it as uniquely "mitochondria-optimal". Avoid marketing claims about Zone 2 for fat burning or longevity. Define the easy zone via the talk test or below VT1/LT1, not via a fixed percentage.

### Gaps
- The full-text pooled estimates from the 115-trial HIIT vs MICT meta-analysis were not retrieved.
- The 2018 ACSM Physical Activity Guidelines (USA, same 150–300 min) and ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription were not retrieved in this session.
- Landmark CRF studies were not retrieved this session: Kodama 2009 *JAMA*; Mandsager 2018 *JAMA Network Open* (no upper limit of benefit for CRF) [UNVERIFIED].
- No rebuttals to Storoschuk 2025 were searched.

## 7. Cycling indoor vs outdoor; cross-training between running and cycling

### Takeaway
VO2max training effects transfer partly between cycling and running, but never more than sport-specific training does **[B]**. Replacing about 20–50% of running load with cycling showed no clear loss of VO2max or running performance in a small 2026 meta-analysis **[B, low certainty]**. Older studies suggest running-specific performance and running economy are less well preserved **[B]**. No evidence was retrieved on indoor vs outdoor cycling physiology.

### Cited Findings
- **Loy, Hoffmann & Holland 1995**, "Effects of cross-training. Transfer of training effects on VO2max between cycling, running and swimming", *Sports Medicine* 19(1):73–85. Some VO2max transfer occurs between modes, but cross-training effects never exceeded those of sport-specific training. Cross-training may be highly beneficial for overall fitness in the general population — [PubMed 7871294](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7871294/)
- Cross-training between cycling and running in untrained females — [PubMed 9062047](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9062047/)
- Specific vs cross-training effects on running performance — [PubMed 7649149](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7649149/)
- Training specificity affects lactate threshold and VO2peak — [PubMed 2228355](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2228355/)
- Runners reach higher VO2max on treadmills and cyclists on cycle ergometers, so the test mode matters — [Marathon Handbook summary](https://marathonhandbook.com/can-cycling-replace-some-of-your-running-heres-what-the-science-says/) (secondary source)
- A 2026 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs found no clear difference in VO2max or running performance when 20–50% of running load was replaced by cycling. Participants were recreationally active, trained or competitive, and trials lasted at least 4 weeks. The authors flagged a small evidence base, outdated protocols and methodological concerns. Note: this finding was reported via search-result synthesis, and its primary citation was not retrieved, so it must be verified — [search context: Rouvy blog](https://rouvy.com/en/blog/does-cycling-help-with-running) (secondary)
- In high-school cross-country runners, elliptical or bike cross-training maintained treadmill VO2max and lactate threshold, but did not preserve running performance — [Marathon Handbook summary](https://marathonhandbook.com/can-cycling-replace-some-of-your-running-heres-what-the-science-says/) (secondary)
- An N = 1 self-experiment found that cycling maintained VO2max but running economy was likely lost — [Marco Altini Substack](https://marcoaltini.substack.com/p/cross-training-experiment-expectations) **[C, anecdotal]**

### Inferences
- **Rule (load accounting across modes) [A for sRPE validity / C for equivalence]:** Use session-RPE × minutes as the common currency across running and cycling. Do not count cycling distance toward running-specific limits such as the longest-run rule, because cycling does not add the same impact load.
- **Rule (substitution) [B/C]:** When volume needs to grow beyond what the user's running tolerance allows (e.g., injury-prone runners), replace easy running volume with cycling, up to about 20–50%. Keep the key running-specific sessions (long run, threshold or intervals) as running if running performance matters.
- **Rule (return from injury) [C]:** Use cycling to maintain CRF during running injuries, then resume running under the single-session rule. Cycling maintains VO2max but not running economy or tissue tolerance.
- **Rule (zones per sport) [B]:** Keep separate zones per sport. HR at a given threshold is typically lower in cycling than in running (convention, [UNVERIFIED] magnitude about 5–10 bpm). Cycling FTP and running CS are not interchangeable.
- **Indoor cycling [C]:** Indoor sessions are suited to structured intervals (controlled power, no traffic). Expect possibly higher HR and RPE at the same power because of heat. Recommend fan cooling. This is not evidence-backed in this session.

### Gaps
- No studies were retrieved on indoor vs outdoor cycling (power, HR, RPE, thermoregulation, smart-trainer accuracy). This needs a dedicated search.
- The primary citation for the 2026 cross-training meta-analysis was not identified. Do not cite it without verification.
- Sport-specific HR offsets between cycling and running were not verified.
